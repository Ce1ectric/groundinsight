    Direct current (f = 0)¶

    f = 0 is an ordinary entry in Network.frequencies and in Fault.scalings, and a direct-current study needs nothing beyond it. The reference chain of this page (dc_chain.py): three earthing systems A – B – C (0.8, 12 and 3.5 Ω) joined by two 10 km spans of an earthing conductor of 0.25 Ω/km, with 1 kA of direct current entering at A and leaving at C:

    def build(conductor_formula, frequency):
    """The chain A - B - C with 1 kA DC entering at A and leaving at C."""
    net = gi.create_network(name="dc_return", frequencies=[frequency])
    for name, z_electrode in (("A", 0.8), ("B", 12.0), ("C", 3.5)):
        electrode = gi.BusType(name=f"electrode_{name}", system_type="Substation",
                               voltage_level=110.0, impedance_formula=repr(z_electrode))
        gi.create_bus(name=name, type=electrode, specific_earth_resistance=100.0,
                      network=net)
    conductor = gi.BranchType(name="conductor", grounding_conductor=True,
                              self_impedance_formula=conductor_formula,
                              mutual_impedance_formula="0")
    gi.create_branch(name="A-B", type=conductor, from_bus="A", to_bus="B",
                     length=10.0, network=net)
    gi.create_branch(name="B-C", type=conductor, from_bus="B", to_bus="C",
                     length=10.0, network=net)
    gi.create_source(name="dc", bus="A", values={frequency: 1000.0}, network=net)
    gi.create_fault(name="F", bus="C", network=net)
    return net


net = build(CARSON, 0.0)
result = gi.run_fault(net, "F")
epr_a = abs(complex(next(b.uepr_freq[0.0] for b in result.buses if b.name == "A")))
z_e = abs(complex(result.reduction_factor.z_earthing[0.0]))

    The earth potential rise at A is 456.621004566 V and the earthing impedance at the fault bus C is 2.073630 Ω; a nodal solve of the three electrodes and two 2.5 Ω conductors by hand gives the same numbers.

    DC needs a section of its own not because the solver is special there but because it is not. \(Y(f)\,\underline{u}(f) = \underline{i}(f)\) has no frequency dependence of its own — \(f\) enters only through the impedances — so a DC solve is an ordinary solve and it is exact. What has to be handled at zero frequency is the other half: what an impedance formula means there, and what happens when the number it produces has no reciprocal.

    Why f = 0 and not f = 0.1 Hz

    Entering a small frequency instead of zero was the established workaround, and for a conductor whose reactance is written as j*2*pi*f*L it was accurate: on the reference chain above 0.1 Hz gives \(456.621004575\ \text{V}\) and 0 Hz gives \(456.621004566\ \text{V}\) — eleven digits. With f = 0 an ordinary frequency, it is not needed.

    For the far more common spelling (0.25 + j*0.6)*l the workaround is wrong, and not marginally: 0.1 Hz keeps the \(6\ \Omega\) of reactance that does not exist at DC and reports 718.54 V instead of 456.62 V — 57 % high — with an earthing impedance \(Z_E = 2.90\ \Omega\) instead of \(2.07\ \Omega\). The reactance is constant, so making the frequency smaller does not make the error smaller.

    ROWS = []
for spelling, formula in (("j*2*pi*f*L", CARSON), ("constant j*X", CONSTANT_X)):
    for frequency in (0.1, 0.0):
        with warnings.catch_warnings():
            # the constant reactance at 0 Hz is announced by a DCLimitWarning
            warnings.simplefilter("ignore", gi.DCLimitWarning)
            res = gi.run_fault(build(formula, frequency), "F")
        bus_a = next(b for b in res.buses if b.name == "A")
        ROWS.append({
            "spelling": spelling,
            "f_Hz": frequency,
            "EPR_A_V": abs(complex(bus_a.uepr_freq[frequency])),
            "Z_E_Ohm": abs(complex(res.reduction_factor.z_earthing[frequency])),
        })

    Three singularities, told apart numerically¶

    At \(f = 0\) three quite different things can happen to a formula string, and in floating point all three arrive as NaN or inf:

    At f = 0 the formula … Example What groundinsight does
    has a removable singularity — \(0\cdot\infty\) with a finite limit Carson's earth-return term \(\omega\ln\!\big(658\sqrt{\rho/f}\,/\,\text{GMR}\big)\), whose limit is 0 because \(\omega\) vanishes linearly while the logarithm diverges logarithmically evaluates the limit and returns it, so the conductor tends to its DC resistance
    has a true pole a series capacitance, \(1/(j\omega C)\to\infty\) returns infinity, which the solver already reads as an open circuit — the correct physics
    genuinely fails a NaN parameter, \(\sqrt{\rho}\) with \(\rho<0\), \(0/0\) raises ValueError naming the formula, the frequency and the parameters, exactly as at any other frequency

    The limit is determined by approaching zero on the decade sequence 1e-6, 1e-7, 1e-8 Hz and comparing two consecutive absolute differences. Measured on the reference cases: an inductance gives \(d_1 = 1.41\cdot10^{-8}\), \(d_2 = 1.41\cdot10^{-9}\) — shrinking, hence convergent — against \(d_1 = 1.43\cdot10^{12}\), \(d_2 = 1.43\cdot10^{13}\) for a capacitance, growing, hence a pole. The criterion is deliberately absolute: a relative one cannot classify a formula whose limit is zero, because there the relative change per decade stays at 90 % forever. The tie-break is biased towards convergent, because mistaking a pole for a limit yields a very large finite impedance that behaves almost like the open circuit it should have been, whereas the opposite mistake would silently disconnect a real earthing conductor.

    A limit found this way is also checked for resolution: if the real part still moves over the last decade, the value at \(10^{-8}\) Hz is only an approximation and a DCLimitWarning says so ("converges only slowly"). A move that is small against the value passes, and so does one that shrinks at least five-fold from one decade to the next — the only test that can judge a limit of zero, such as the earth-return resistance \(\pi^2 10^{-4} f\) of a Carson mutual impedance: its change per decade is 90 % of its value, but that change shrinks ten-fold from one decade to the next. sqrt(f) (3.2-fold) and log(f) (not at all) are reported, and since the ratio does not depend on the length, all branches of one type get the same verdict.

    A real part that runs to \(-\infty\) — a log(f) term without a factor \(f\) — is none of the three. It is the limit of negative resistances, not the open circuit \(+\infty\) of a pole, and it is refused for every impedance that is inverted, like a negative real part. Accepted, it would read as an open circuit, because \(1/(-\infty) = 0\).

    A finite reactance has no reading at DC¶

    (0.25 + j*0.6)*l reports the same reactance at every frequency, zero included — and at DC a reactance can only vanish (\(j\omega L \to 0\)) or be infinite (\(1/(j\omega C)\to\infty\)). A finite non-zero one is a statement about nothing. The 0 Hz bin therefore takes the real part, drops the reactance and emits a DCLimitWarning that quotes \(X/R\) and the remedy; all other frequencies are untouched. Write the reactance as j*2*pi*f*L and nothing is dropped and nothing warns. The warning quotes the ratio rather than the two values because the ratio is length-invariant and therefore identical for every branch sharing a BranchType — which lets Python's default warning filter collapse a hundred-branch network into a single line.

    The fallback applies to impedance formulas only. The R_formula, L_formula and C_formula fields consumed by the state-space transient solver are real at every frequency by contract, so a complex value there is reported as an error rather than repaired.

    An ideal bond is a short circuit, and a short circuit has no admittance¶

    A purely inductive element — a short bonding conductor modelled as j*2*pi*f*L*l, with no resistance — is an exact zero at DC. That is correct physics, and it is also a number the nodal formulation cannot invert. Up to v0.4.0 both solvers responded by dropping such an element, i.e. by modelling a short circuit as a disconnection. On the reference chain (\(Z_A = 0.8\ \Omega\), \(Z_B = 12\ \Omega\), \(Z_C = 3.5\ \Omega\), bond A–B purely inductive, \(R_{BC} = 0.35\ \Omega\), 1 kA at A, fault at C) the correct answer is \(u_A = u_B = 57.07\ \text{V}\), \(u_C = -266.30\ \text{V}\); what came back was \(800.00\), \(-2649.84\) and \(-2727.13\ \text{V}\) — wrong by factors of 14.0, 46.4 and 10.2, with the sign of bus B reversed, because bus A had been cut off from the network and reported its own electrode instead of the parallel combination.

    Such an element is now given a finite stand-in at the 0 Hz bin only:

    \[ Z_{\text{sub}} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}}\; Z_{\min} = 1.4901\cdot10^{-8}\; Z_{\min}, \]

    where \(Z_{\min}\) is the smallest finite non-zero impedance magnitude the network carries at that frequency. Tying the stand-in to \(Z_{\min}\) was chosen by measurement against analytically node-merged reference networks; it costs a relative error of about \(10^{-5}\) in the worst case observed, \(1.6\cdot10^{-9}\) on the chain above, and \(8.3\cdot10^{-8}\) at the DC bin of the corresponding transient run — better than modelling the bond as an explicit \(10^{-8}\ \Omega\) resistance. Every use is announced by a DCLimitWarning naming the elements, the substitute and the reference it was scaled to.

    When to merge two buses instead. The exact model of an ideal bond is one node, not two, and the stand-in is an approximation of that merge. Prefer the merge — one bus whose impedance is the parallel combination of the two electrodes — whenever the difference in earth potential rise across the bond is part of the result you report (across an ideal bond it is zero, and whatever the stand-in produces there is numerical residue), or when the bonded buses carry impedances many orders of magnitude away from \(Z_{\min}\). For the usual question — the earth potential rise of the bonded group and the current leaving it — the substitution is accurate to the digits above and needs no change to the model. Giving the bond a small but honest resistance works equally well and removes the warning.

    Phase conductors follow the same rule. A phase_impedance_formula without a resistive part is an ideal short at 0 Hz as well. When the phase network is solved (phase_current_mode="auto"), such a conductor receives the same stand-in, announced by the same DCLimitWarning; \(Z_{\min}\) stays the smallest impedance of the earthing network, so the earthing results of a network with a DC short do not move because its phase conductors are declared. Above 0 Hz a phase impedance that is zero, too small to invert or has a negative real part is refused with a ValueError naming the branch, as for every other element. One consequence is worth knowing: where all parallel routes of the phase network are ideal shorts at DC, the model has no DC split of its own, and every conductor carrying the same stand-in splits the current as if all of them had one and the same impedance, whatever their lengths. Declare the DC resistance in phase_impedance_formula (e.g. R*l + j*2*pi*f*L*l) when the DC split between routes matters.

    Every transient study contains DC¶

    An rfft frequency grid always contains a 0 Hz bin, so every transient run evaluates every formula at zero frequency, whether or not the study is about DC, and there is no f = 0.1 Hz workaround on that path. The FFT solver therefore follows the rules of the stationary solve: a Carson-type conductor takes its DC limit, an ideal bond the stand-in. The state-space solver works from the lumped R/L/C fields and never inverts an impedance, so it needs no stand-in and serves as the independent cross-check: for the bond of the example notebook its settled DC state agrees with the node-merged solution to \(10^{-13}\), and it keeps the two bonded buses at one potential.

    What a source and a fault need at DC¶

    • Current source. values needs a 0.0 key: values={0.0: 1000.0}. Without it the source contributes nothing at DC, announced once per run (InputWarning, code SOURCE_FREQUENCY_GAP). Give it a real number — at zero frequency the imaginary part is not a phase shift.
    • Voltage source. voltage and source_impedance both need a 0.0 entry; a network solved at 0 Hz without one is rejected at the solve. The 0.0 entry of source_impedance is the DC resistance of the source: the values are numbers, not formulas, so nothing drops a reactance there for you. A zero source_impedance is rejected when the source is constructed.
    • Fault. scalings does not need a 0.0 key: a frequency without a key is scaled with 1.0, i.e. the sources contribute in full at DC. Give 0.0: 0.0 to exclude the DC bin from a fault. A mapping that names some network frequencies but not others is announced once per run (InputWarning, code SCALINGS_INCOMPLETE); scalings may also be left out entirely, which means 1.0 everywhere.
    • IEC 60909 characteristics. i_k_a, r_to_x and kappa describe the DC offset and thermal equivalent of an alternating short-circuit current. They do not enter the solve (see Objects) and have no meaning in a pure DC study; leave them unset there.
    • Mixed studies. frequencies=[0.0, 50.0] is legal and the two are independent: the DC handling is confined to the 0 Hz bin, and a study carrying both returns the same 50 Hz answer, bit for bit, as one carrying 50 Hz alone.
