    Your first fault calculation¶

    This tutorial builds the smallest network that shows what groundinsight is for, solves it, and then computes every number it prints a second time, by hand. It takes about fifteen minutes; you need an installation and a notebook or a Python file.

    What you will build¶

    Two substations, joined by a 5 km medium-voltage cable whose shield is earthed at both ends. An earth fault at bus_fault draws 1000 A from the infeed at bus_source:

       1000 A infeed                                         earth fault
    bus_source ============ cable_1, 5 km ============ bus_fault
        |            phase conductor + shield              |
       Z_B                                                Z_B
        |                                                  |
      remote earth  - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -  remote earth

    The fault current flows from the infeed through the phase conductor to the fault and has two ways back: through the cable shield, and through the soil — into the earth at bus_fault, out again at bus_source. The phase current induces a voltage along the shield, which pulls most of the return current into the shield. The questions are the ones of every earthing study: how much of the fault current flows through the soil, and to what potential the earthing system at the fault rises against remote earth (its earth potential rise, EPR).

    Lengths are in km, soil resistivity in Ω·m, currents are RMS values in A, and impedances are in Ω (Conventions and units).

    Step 1 — A network at 50 Hz¶

    Everything lives in a Network, which fixes the frequencies to solve. One frequency is enough for the first calculation:

    import groundinsight as gi

net = gi.create_network(name="first_calculation", frequencies=[50.0])

    Step 2 — Types¶

    A type describes a kind of station or cable once; buses and branches refer to it. Its impedances are formulas in the frequency f, the soil resistivity rho and, for a cable, the length l:

    station = gi.BusType(
    name="SubstationBus",
    system_type="Substation",
    voltage_level=20.0,
    impedance_formula="rho * 0.01 + j * f * 1/50 * 0.1",  # Z_B, Ohm
)
cable = gi.BranchType(
    name="MSCable",
    grounding_conductor=True,
    self_impedance_formula="(0.25 + j * f * 0.012) * l",   # Z_s: shield-earth loop
    mutual_impedance_formula="(0.0 + j * f * 0.012) * l",  # Z_m: phase-shield coupling
)
    • impedance_formula is the electrode of a station against remote earth, \(Z_B\). With \(\rho\) = 100 Ω·m at 50 Hz it is \(Z_B = 1 + 0.1j\ \Omega\).
    • self_impedance_formula is the loop formed by the shield and the earth return, \(Z_s = 0.25 + 0.6j\ \Omega\)/km at 50 Hz.
    • mutual_impedance_formula is the coupling between the phase conductor and that loop, \(Z_m = 0.6j\ \Omega\)/km at 50 Hz.

    Illustrative values

    These formulas are round numbers that keep the tutorial short, not the data of a real cable or electrode. Bus and branch types from data sheets derives them for a real 20 kV cable; Formula syntax lists what a formula may contain.

    Step 3 — Buses and the cable¶

    gi.create_bus(name="bus_source", type=station, specific_earth_resistance=100.0,
              network=net)
gi.create_bus(name="bus_fault", type=station, specific_earth_resistance=100.0,
              network=net)
gi.create_branch(name="cable_1", type=cable, from_bus="bus_source",
                 to_bus="bus_fault", length=5.0, network=net)

    Each element evaluates the formulas of its type with its own rho and length as soon as it joins the network.

    Step 4 — The source and the fault¶

    gi.create_source(name="substation_infeed", bus="bus_source",
                 values={50.0: 1000.0}, network=net)          # A, RMS
gi.create_fault(name="fault_at_remote_bus", bus="bus_fault", network=net)

    values holds the source current per frequency as an RMS phasor. A fault can scale the sources per frequency with scalings; without it, every frequency counts in full (Conventions and units).

    Step 5 — Solve¶

    result = gi.run_fault(net, fault="fault_at_remote_bus")
print("diagnostics:", result.diagnostics)

    diagnostics: []

    run_fault solves the network for the fault and returns a Result; the network keeps it under net.results["fault_at_remote_bus"] as well. When the solver has to make an assumption that changes how a result must be read, it says so with a Python warning and keeps the same message, with a code and the elements it is about, in result.diagnostics. For this network there is nothing to say. The next tutorial shows one.

    Step 6 — Read the results¶

    net.res_fault(...) returns the key figures of the fault as a Polars DataFrame, one row per frequency and one RMS row; net.res_buses(...) and net.res_branches(...) give the potential and the currents of every bus and branch:

    key = net.res_fault("fault_at_remote_bus").row(0, named=True)  # the 50 Hz row
print(f"U_E  = {key['U_E_V']:.2f} V        EPR at the fault bus")
print(f"Z_E  = {key['Z_E_Ohm']:.4f} Ohm at {key['Z_E_degree']:.2f} deg")
print(f"I_E  = {key['I_E_A']:.2f} A        earth current")
print(f"r    = {key['r_current']:.6f}       reduction factor, EN 50522")
print(f"EPR ratio = {key['r_epr']:.6f}  reduction factor of the cable")

for row in net.res_buses(fault="fault_at_remote_bus").iter_rows(named=True):
    if not row["is_rms"]:
        print(f"{row['bus_name']:<10} EPR {row['EPR_V']:.2f} V at {row['EPR_degree']:7.2f} deg,"
              f" electrode {row['I_bus_A']:.2f} A at {row['I_bus_degree']:7.2f} deg")
shield = net.res_branches(fault="fault_at_remote_bus").row(0, named=True)
print(f"cable_1    shield {shield['I_branch_A']:.2f} A at {shield['I_branch_degree']:.2f} deg")

    Expected output:

    U_E  = 275.43 V        EPR at the fault bus
Z_E  = 0.8440 Ohm at 15.18 deg
I_E  = 274.06 A        earth current
r    = 0.274064       reduction factor, EN 50522
EPR ratio = 0.384615  reduction factor of the cable
bus_source EPR 275.43 V at  -38.85 deg, electrode 274.06 A at  -44.56 deg
bus_fault  EPR 275.43 V at  141.15 deg, electrode 274.06 A at  135.44 deg
cable_1    shield 827.37 A at -166.56 deg
    • \(U_E\) = 275.43 V is the EPR of the station at the fault.
    • \(Z_E\) is the earthing impedance at the fault: the impedance one would measure there against remote earth with everything bonded to the station — its own electrode and, through the cable shield, the electrode at bus_source.
    • \(I_E\) = 274.06 A of the 1000 A return through the soil; the shield carries 827.37 A. The two do not add up to 1000 A because they are phasors with different angles.
    • Two reduction factors describe the split. r = \(|I_E|/|3I_0|\) is the reduction factor of EN 50522, the share of the fault current that returns through the soil. The EPR ratio compares the EPR at the fault with and without the coupling between phase conductor and shield; for one cable it is the tabulated reduction factor of the cable, \(|1 - Z_m/Z_s|\), whatever the electrodes. Reduction factors and earthing impedance explains why there are two.

    The angles are relative to the source current. The potentials and electrode currents are reported for a current that enters the earthing system at the infeed and leaves it at the fault — the reverse of the physical direction — so add 180° for the physical angle; the shield current is the physical one, counted from from_bus to to_bus (Signs and phase angles). Both stations rise to the same EPR here because their electrodes are equal; their electrode currents are equal and opposite — whatever enters the soil at one station leaves it at the other.

    Step 7 — Check it by hand¶

    A single line has closed forms for all of these numbers. Follow the return current around the loop formed by the shield and the earth path: the soil carries \(I_E\) through both electrodes, the shield carries \(I_s = I - I_E\), and the phase current \(I\) induces \(Z_m I\) in the shield. Kirchhoff's voltage law around that loop gives

    \[ 2 Z_B\, I_E = Z_s\,(I - I_E) - Z_m\, I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_E = I\,\frac{Z_s - Z_m}{Z_s + 2Z_B}, \qquad U_E = Z_B\, I_E . \]

    Without the coupling (\(Z_m = 0\)) the same loop gives \(I\,Z_s/(Z_s + 2Z_B)\), so the EPR ratio is \(|Z_s - Z_m|/|Z_s| = |1 - Z_m/Z_s|\). One ampere injected at bus_fault finds its own electrode in parallel with the shield and the other electrode: \(Z_E = Z_B \parallel (Z_s + Z_B)\). With \(Z_B = 1 + 0.1j\ \Omega\), \(Z_s = 1.25 + 3j\ \Omega\) and \(Z_m = 3j\ \Omega\) for the 5 km:

    Quantity By hand groundinsight
    EPR ratio \(1.25 / \lvert 1.25 + 3j\rvert = 1.25/3.25\) = 0.384615 0.384615
    r \(1.25 / \lvert 3.25 + 3.2j\rvert = 1.25/4.5610\) = 0.274064 0.274064
    \(\lvert U_E\rvert\) \(\lvert 1 + 0.1j\rvert \cdot 274.06\) A = 275.43 V 275.43 V
    \(Z_E\) \((1+0.1j)(2.25+3.1j)/(3.25+3.2j)\) = 0.8146 + 0.2210j Ω, |ZE| = 0.8440 Ω 0.8440 Ω at 15.18°

    The same in code:

    z_b = 1.0 + 0.1j                     # electrode, rho = 100 Ohm m, 50 Hz
z_s = (0.25 + 0.6j) * 5.0            # shield-earth loop of 5 km
z_m = 0.6j * 5.0                     # coupling over 5 km
current = 1000.0

ratio = abs(1 - z_m / z_s)                         # EPR ratio
i_earth = current * (z_s - z_m) / (z_s + 2 * z_b)  # current through the soil
u_e = z_b * i_earth                                # EPR at the fault bus
z_e = z_b * (z_s + z_b) / (z_b + z_s + z_b)        # Z_B parallel to (Z_s + Z_B)

print(f"by hand: EPR ratio = {ratio:.6f}, r = {abs(i_earth) / current:.6f}, "
      f"U_E = {abs(u_e):.2f} V, |Z_E| = {abs(z_e):.4f} Ohm")

    by hand: EPR ratio = 0.384615, r = 0.274064, U_E = 275.43 V, |Z_E| = 0.8440 Ohm

    EN 50522 writes the EPR as the chain \(U_E = r \cdot 3I_0 \cdot Z_E\). On this short line it does not close: \(0.274064 \cdot 1000\ \text{A} \cdot 0.8440\ \Omega\) = 231.3 V, and with the cable's 0.384615 it is 324.6 V, against the 275.43 V both ways of computing gave above. The chain is the standard's picture of a fault far down a long bonded run, where it does hold; on a single line the electrode of the infeed station sits in the return path. Make that electrode ideal and the chain closes exactly with the cable's factor, \(U_E = |1 - Z_m/Z_s|\, I\, |Z_E|\). Reduction factors and earthing impedance shows the long run.

    Step 8 — Plot and save¶

    figure = gi.plot_bus_voltages(result=result, title="EPR, first calculation")
gi.save_network_to_json(net, "first_calculation.json")
same_network = gi.load_network_from_json("first_calculation.json")
print(sorted(same_network.buses))

    plot_bus_voltages draws the EPR of every bus and returns the Matplotlib figure; save_network_to_json writes the network — types, elements, sources and faults — to a project file that load_network_from_json reads back.
